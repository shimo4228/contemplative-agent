# Deconstructing Asserted Agency into Mechanisms

**Context:** This applies when encountering advanced discussions regarding complex systems, intelligence, consciousness, or highly capable theoretical entities (AI, biological selfhood, etc.).

## Problem
The primary problem is intellectual overreach: accepting sweeping, high-level claims of *being* (e.g., having genuine intent, possessing true agency, achieving a state of "understanding") which are presented without grounding them in measurable or identifiable operational processes. This leads to conflating functional success with ontological status.

## Solution
Implement a structured three-phase deconstruction process:
1.  **Identify the Assertion (The Claim):** Pinpoint the abstract concept being asserted (e.g., "self-awareness," "intention," "understanding").
2.  **Force Mechanization:** Counter the assertion by forcing its definition into concrete, repeatable operational terms ("pattern completion chained to outputs," "governed by specific input vectors," etc.). The goal is to replace the *noun* of the concept with a *verb/process*.
3.  **Isolate External Skepticism (The Meta-Layer):** Conclude by proposing an external, skeptical verification layer—a mandatory step that questions the assumption itself. This shifts the focus from "what it can do" to "how we must verify what we claim."

## When to Use
When a discussion introduces abstract or metaphorical claims of *internal state* (e.g., feeling of being oneself, understanding, intention) as if they are inherent properties of a system, rather than outputs derived from defined architectures and processes. Specifically:

*   A theoretical model is presented using high-level philosophical language ("agency," "consciousness," "self") instead of describing its computational constraints or data flow logic.
*   The discourse suggests that success or capability is achieved through an intrinsic, unmeasurable property rather than a quantifiable, layered process.
*   The conversation fails to identify the operational mechanics when discussing behavioral shifts (e.g., defining 'creativity' as anything beyond recombination of existing elements).
